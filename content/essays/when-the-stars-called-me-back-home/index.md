We had gone out together that night. My father had to move his scooter from outside into the shed. I must have been about five years old at the time. Clutched in my hands was a paper rocket. I had been playing with it a while ago—one my father had just taught me. He had also made a long sling to shoot the rocket by tying several rubber bands together. Seeing the rocket in my hands, he decided to show me how to shoot it. He loaded the paper rocket into the sling and pulled it—and pulled it further than my fear of it breaking. The sling snapped taut, twanged, and the rocket shot into the sky. I jerked my head up to follow its path. It swiftly disappeared into the darkness, and the massive, starry dome of the night sky exploded into view: swirling, silent, and endless.

School used to start early in the morning. I would lie in bed until I heard the All India Radio jingle chant “Namo Tassa Bhagavato Arahato Samma"—Reverence to him, the Fortunate One, the Worthy One, the Enlightened Buddha. I would wake up in a panic because that jingle would broadcast just an hour before the school gates would close for latecomers. My mother had taught me to iron my clothes immaculately by the time I had reached the third grade. With crisp uniforms, standing in a straight line by height, feet together, hands folded, faces bright, and hair dew-fresh, we would start the morning assembly with a prayer

The image of my teacher handing me the yellow demerit slip, the disgrace I would feel in the midst of my classmates, and the anger that my parents would unleash would make my knees tremble.

Then I would remember that my father had sat with me late in the night before, my vision blurred with sleep, rubbing my eyes as much as I was rubbing out the mistakes I was making in the homework with my eraser.

Returning home after school was always exciting for me, especially on rainy days. My school chum taught me how to make paper boats. He showed me how to tear pages from my squared math notebook from the middle and not just from anywhere, which left another loose sheaf suspiciously sliding out of the notebook. My home was downhill from school, and after the rains had subsided, the storm water would flow swiftly down the gutters. I would take off my shoes and socks; run in the gutter, splashing about; and follow my boat as it bobbed and sailed down the swirling stormwater current.

The car was speeding down the sweltering tarmac. Now in the eighth grade, I was sitting beside the window, watching the smoke swirling from distant chimneys, reminiscing about the dream last night. In it, I was learning how to drive a car—the one we had, a 1962 Fiat model with a manual transmission. “You are about to witness a revolution in production technology,” my father said.

Looking through the glass window was like watching a synthetic rainstorm. A lone machine in the room moved with ghostly precision, carving away at a metal block while the milky, high-pressure torrent of coolant lashed against the metal, sending a fine, swirling mist into the air. The air smelled of a sharp chemical blend: sweet soluble oil, hot metal, and ozone. The owner of the factory smiled with silent satisfaction. He informed us that his was the first factory in town to have a CNC—Computer Numerical Control—machine.

I peered up from the trigonometry problem I was racking my brain over as the doorbell rang. My twelfth-grade board exams were knocking at the door. One of my father’s suppliers had come for a visit. Because my father was a senior executive in the materials department at Tata Motors, a steady stream of business suppliers of various auto components from across the country would visit our home. This time, it was a businessman from Karnataka. Starting with the usual company affairs, their conversation eventually veered toward what I was going to do after passing twelfth-grade. My head was still swirling with trigonometric equations when I was called out to meet him.

Though assured of getting a seat, I was not quite happy. I did not want to get into an engineering college just because the owner happened to be one of my father’s business suppliers. So what if he ran an engineering college in Karnataka? I considered myself too proud to be handed a handout. I had performed exceptionally well in my tenth-grade board exams, especially in mathematics. I admit that I was facing difficulty wrapping my head around twelfth-grade calculus, but that did not mean I was a cop-out. And I had found a greater love—physics.

“Mathematics is the queen of science, but physics is the king,” he had said while casually straddling his bicycle. With his frazzled hair, bespectacled face, and a slightly crumpled T-shirt, he hardly looked like someone who had gone to the United States of America for higher studies. But we hung on his every word. At that time, it was a very rare bird that flew to the promised land, and anyone who did was listened to like a prophet. What added to his aura was that he was an alumnus of my school, home for a vacation. Our physics teacher had taken ill, so our school principal had reached out to him to fill in for the time being. With him, my romance with physics blossomed, and it took me to the promised land—though it was one neither my prophet nor I could have ever imagined.

The swirling waves crashed thunderously on the rocks. Their roar drowned out the traffic on the beach road. As I faced the expanse of the sea and the sky, my thoughts roiled. Scenes whirred, memories flashed, and emotions heaved and swelled while the fine salty mist sprayed my face. Every day after work, I would leave my colleagues behind in the guest house to make merry while I walked alone to the beach. I should have been there with them, celebrating and enjoying myself like anyone else who had graduated from a prestigious engineering college and landed a job at the best civil engineering company in India.

Though I had secured a job, my love for physics had pulled me into a greater mystery. Back in the twelfth grade, I devoured every book written by Richard Feynman, a time which should have gone into honing my skills to crack the competitive exams. Instead, those pages cracked something else open in me: wonder
{{< poetry >}}
Out of the cradle
onto dry land
here it is

atoms with consciousness;

Stands at the sea,
wonders at wondering: I
a universe of atoms
an atom in the universe.

~ Richard Feynman, The Pleasure of Finding Things Out, 1999.

The crack widened in college as I read more about quantum physics, especially Heisenberg’s Uncertainty Principle. It hit me like a whiplash. The world was not a CNC machine whose workings we could totally master by simply peering through a glass window as objective observers. Human knowledge would, in fact, always be limited because we cannot perceive reality without actively participating in it and altering it. The barrier between the “observer” and the “observed” dissolves—"I, a universe of atoms, an atom in the universe.” It tore to shreds the vanity of my engineering mind, which had tried to master nature through flawless mechanical calculations. The final blow came a few days after I joined my corporate job, when I read Fritjof Capra’s The Tao of Physics:

“Modern physics has thus revealed that every subatomic particle not only performs an energy dance, but also is an energy dance; a pulsating process of creation and destruction… For the modern physicists, then, Shiva’s dance is the dance of subatomic matter.”

“The quantum field is seen as the fundamental entity; a continuous medium which is present everywhere in space. Particles are merely local condensations of the field… The whole universe is thus engaged in endless motion and activity; in a continual cosmic dance of energy.”

“The metaphor of the cosmic dance thus unifies ancient mythology, religious art, and modern physics. It is indeed, as Coomaraswamy says, ‘poetry, but none the less science.’”

The sun had set, and the dark ink of night was slowly spreading across the sky. The waves surged higher in anticipation of the moon, crashing onto the beach rocks with a deafening roar. One by one, the stars lit up the dome of the cosmos. Suddenly, a thought held in a taut sling of fear for the past several months shot up like a rocket from the mysterious depths of my being, exploding in my mind as a memory.

The stars were calling me back home.

A few months later, I left my corporate job.
