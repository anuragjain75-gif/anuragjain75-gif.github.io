## The Question
Sitting alone in my room, I was in a peculiar ferment. My third year in engineering college had begun. Campus interviews were just around the corner, and I had no idea what I wanted. For my friends, stepping into the corporate world felt like an opening into their world of dreams. I felt boxed in. Whatever this turmoil was, I was trying to work it out alone, with all the wisdom a twenty-year-old engineering student could muster.

Books were my only holy grail. In my civil engineering lecture classes, I would hide a Krishnamurti or an Ayn Rand or a Richard Bach under my notebook. Once, while reading Paul Brunton’s Wisdom of the Overself, I became so absorbed that I nearly forgot to meet my girlfriend on my birthday. I desperately tried to find in the labyrinthine equations of solid mechanics and structural design something of what I encountered in those pages. Howard Roark gave wings to my imagination, but I missed the skies in which Jonathan Livingston Seagull flew. In our classes of concrete technology, I could not find my Walden Pond. Amidst steel, concrete, and road designs, I found reprieve in geology. Our classes were held in a laboratory filled with fascinating samples of stones collected from different sites. I also liked survey engineering, taught outdoors with the sun gleaming down on us and the wind playing with our hair.

We also had a smattering of courses in economics, political theory, management, and communication skills. One day I heard from Pigeon—our nickname for the economics professor because he looked and spoke like one—about an economist called E.F. Schumacher and his book Small Is Beautiful. It was the only thing I remember from his lectures, though I would pick up the book itself a decade later.

As one would expect, I chose English literature when it was offered as an elective in my third year. Our English professor was known for being eccentric, probably because he did not speak the language of mathematics. He spoke of people, relationships, and emotions and often punctuated his lectures with mischievous sexual innuendos.

In one of his classes I happened to ask a question that I no longer remember. After the class ended and we drifted out for lunch, a classmate who had never spoken to me in the previous three years—and never spoke to me again afterwards—walked up to me and asked:

“Who are you, man? What are you going to do in your life? ”

## The Fire of Inquiry
The Buddha sat right on the top.

I was staring down at the diagram my girlfriend had drawn with a stick on the ground. I had always considered myself the philosopher in the relationship, forever recommending books and introducing new ideas. One evening, she turned the tables on me. “Would you like to hear my philosophy of life?” she asked.

Using a twig, she drew a rectangle on the ground and divided it into four segments. The lower three were occupied by different classes of people, each exercising power or influence over those below them. I followed her argument with interest, but my attention kept returning to the empty box at the top.

“And who occupies that?” I finally asked.

“The Buddha,” she said.

The answer struck me like a thunderclap. The next thing I remember is rushing back to my hostel room. It was the first sleepless night of my life.

The Buddha was not entirely new to me. A memory surfaced from my childhood. In my fifth-grade history textbook there was a black-and-white image of him seated beneath a peepal tree, eyes half closed, a halo encircling his head. I remember staring at that image and being struck by a single word: enlightenment. Something about it had stirred me then. That night, after my girlfriend’s pronouncement, the memory returned with unexpected force.

Wealth, success, and fame did not particularly intimidate me. Youth had given me an almost reckless confidence that, if I truly wanted them, I could pursue any worldly ambition to its end. But I had no idea what the Buddha had attained. Beyond the word "enlightenment," I knew nothing. What had he discovered that allowed him to sit like that while the world whirled around him? Was it merely a state of mind, or something altogether different? The question followed me deep into the night.

By the time the morning star faded into the growing light, I had resolved. When I met my girlfriend later that morning, I told her that I was determined to find out what the Buddha had found, though I had no intention of renouncing the world or disappearing into a forest. But I knew almost nothing about the destination. All I possessed was a single word: enlightenment.

Standing on the very soil where I stood, two thousand five hundred years ago, another young warrior had found himself in the grip of an existential crisis far more dramatic than my own. On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, Arjuna had cast aside his bow and fallen to his knees in despair, turning to Krishna to guide him through the labyrinth of his confusion.

In the Regional Engineering College, Kurukshetra, another young man was wrestling with his own questions. There were no armies arrayed before me, no conches blowing, no kingdom hanging in the balance. Yet the turmoil felt real enough. And just as Krishna had appeared before Arjuna on the battlefield, another Krishna now entered my life.

His name was Jiddu Krishnamurti.

He arrived as my charioteer, but in the form of books.

One day, as I was strolling along an aisle in the library, a book unexpectedly tipped and fell across the shelf. It bore the face of a man of magnetic intensity; his liquid black eyes gathered in a gaze of immense concentration, while the title riveted me: The Network of Thought.

The first page read:

>*Thought is the common factor of all mankind. There is no Eastern thought, or Western thought; there is only the common capacity to think, whether one is utterly poor or most sophisticated, living in an affluent society. Whether a surgeon, a carpenter, a labourer in the field, or a great poet, thought is the common factor of all of us. We do not seem to realize that thought is the common factor that binds us all. You think according to your capacity, to your energy, your experience and knowledge; another thinks differently according to his experience and conditioning. We are all caught in this network of thought. This is a fact, indisputable and actual.*

Holy Christ! Why had I never thought this before?

And then he went on:

>*Thought has done marvellous things to help man but it has also brought about great destruction and terror in the world. We have to understand the nature and the movement of thought; why you think in a certain way; why you cling to certain forms of thought; why you hold on to certain experiences; why thought has never understood the nature of death. We have to examine the very structure of thought.*

What?

It was as though Krishnamurti had asked me to look through the other end of the telescope. Until then I had been looking only outward. It had never occurred to me to look at what looks.

This was a total inversion of everything I had encountered so far in my life. The moment I began looking inward, a cataclysm seemed to erupt within me. It felt as though someone had switched on a 440-volt dynamo inside my organism and forgotten how to turn it off. Explosions of white light disrupted my sleep for months. I could never quite return to my easy and carefree ways again.

Only much later did I encounter traditions that had a name for such things. They called it Kundalini. Krishnamurti himself underwent something similar, describing it in detail in Krishnamurti’s Notebook. He called it “the process,” and eventually I began calling it the same.

I wanted to give all my time and energy to the process unfolding within me. Life had other plans. Marriage, responsibility, and the practicalities of earning a livelihood led me into the corporate world as an engineer with a civil construction company.

I was posted to a far-flung town in the interior of Rajasthan, a state dominated by the Indian Thar Desert. Someone else might have felt despondent. I was wonder-struck. Even in its starkness, the desert landscape seemed a poetry written in stone and scrubland.

My work involved erecting transmission line towers across vast stretches of uninhabited terrain and scattered villages. I encountered the rural life of the desert unmediated by television, newspapers, or any discourse of the media. Since I was not confined to a cubicle under the gaze of a superior, I spent much of my time traversing the landscape itself. With not a soul in sight and only an eagle circling overhead, I climbed hills, walked through dry riverbeds, and wandered across the open countryside.

Along the way I passed village women dressed in vibrant ghagras, cholis and flowing odhnis. Their colours and intricate craftsmanship rioted against the subdued palette of the desert. In that slowness, vastness, and unfiltered beauty, my self-inquiry blazed like the desert sun overhead.

One day my immediate superior called me into his office.

“See how you can trick him by offering the lowest compensation.”

He was referring to a farmer whose field would soon host one of our transmission towers.

I had become a favourite among the village folk. I ate sitting on the ground with them, shoveled alongside the labourers and occasionally drove their tractors. They responded with immense warmth, often bringing specially prepared traditional food for me. The thought of deceiving them troubled me deeply.

As it turned out, I didn’t have to play any tricks. The farmer was far more perceptive than we had assumed. He told me he had spent several years working in Mumbai before returning to the village because he preferred this life.

“The wires hiss when it rains,” he said, more as an observation than a complaint.

I looked around. His field shimmered green beneath the harsh sun. Soon a transmission tower would rise from the middle of it, a metal monster planted among tender stalks of grain.

The city dweller who flipped a switch would never see what I was seeing. The farmer, in turn, would never experience the convenience that electricity brought to distant homes. Only the tower was being gifted to him: not the electricity.

I resigned from the corporate world eight months later and never returned.

Rajasthan had opened a wound in me. Until then my questions had been directed inward. Now another question appeared with equal force: What was happening to villages, communities, and ways of life in the name of progress?

## Education of the Heart
“Where are the children in your consciousness?”

My principal’s eyes pierced through his thin-rimmed spectacles as he reprimanded me. Feeling suffocated, I had decided to bunk school and spend the day at the beach.

When I joined The School, one of the schools run by the Krishnamurti Foundation, self-inquiry remained my primary concern. After leaving the corporate world, I had imagined finding a quiet job — perhaps even becoming a librarian surrounded by Krishnamurti’s books — while devoting the greater part of my energy to inquiry. Teaching had never crossed my mind. Yet the only opening available was as a teacher, and so, somewhat reluctantly, I accepted.

My principal’s question jolted me out of my cave.

I was no longer erecting metal towers across distant landscapes. There were lives under my care. Nor was this just any school. In The School, education was not merely a passport to a better job.

Krishnamurti often said that one learns in the mirror of relationship. Anger, hurt, jealousy, and fear arise in a relationship—to another person, an object, an idea, or even an image of oneself. Until then I had largely imagined inquiry as a solitary affair. In The School, I encountered the possibility that inquiry could also be shared—unfolding through dialogue, relationship, and the daily encounter with other human beings, including children.

I flung myself wholeheartedly into the task of educating children, sharing with them the space of inquiry that had become central to my own life. Naturally, I was curious to see what became of them after they left school.

What I saw was not entirely heartening.

After years of questioning, dialogue, and inquiry, most of them seemed to drift back into the familiar patterns of society. The careers, ambitions, and lifestyles they pursued were often indistinguishable from those of their peers elsewhere. Despite all the assumptions The School encouraged them to question—about success, status, consumption, class, and the environment—very few chose lives that were radically different.

Another fact troubled me. Because of the high fee structure, most of the children came from families that were already rich in resources and opportunities. The question began to gnaw at me: if inquiry, freedom, and holistic education were valuable, why should they remain the privilege of a relatively small and affluent section of society?

The painted sign on the school’s metal gate boldly proclaimed:

NEEV Vidyalaya — Where Education Is Not A Business

Every morning it welcomed children from economically weaker families in the surrounding township and nearby villages.

When I founded NEEV—New Education and Environment Visions, a non-profit—after leaving The School, the inquiry that had once been directed solely at me had spilled beyond the boundaries of my own life. It had entered classrooms, communities, and the difficult question of how human beings might live together. NEEV was involved not only in education but also in livelihood, health, and cultural development in slums and villages.

## Seeing History
A couple of years later, Shastri ji, as I fondly called him, entered my life—wearing a pair of slippers, dressed in rumpled clothes, and carrying a cloth bag slung casually over one shoulder.

By the time he entered my life, my work through NEEV had already taught me something important. Distributing blankets and providing medicines and educational opportunities could certainly alleviate suffering. It also brought appreciation. Articles about our work began appearing in newspapers and magazines. Yet, beneath that quiet satisfaction, I could not shake the feeling that we were still not addressing something fundamental. What was the root of poverty and injustice? Why did the divisions in society that my girlfriend had once sketched so casually on the ground continue to reproduce themselves?

For Shastri Ji, the answer was capitalism.

“Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun.” That was Mao’s answer to overthrowing capitalism. Shastri ji had never wielded a gun in his half century as a Marxist. His weapons were words and ideas.

Traveling with him to workers’ rallies, farmers’ meetings, and gatherings of social activists and college students, the comfortable bubble of my middle-class upbringing slowly dissolved. He trained me to see the dynamics of class in every sphere of life. More importantly, he placed books in my hands that revealed the historical evolution of capitalism and communism.

When I read Leo Huberman’s Man’s Worldly Goods, the scales fell from my eyes. For the first time, I understood the meaning of the word worldview. It was as though my old Newtonian way of seeing the world — as a fixed stage of absolute space and time upon which events merely unfolded — had shattered. In its place emerged something that felt closer to the vision of quantum mechanics. Reality no longer appeared fixed but as an infinite field of possibilities, continuously opening in every moment.

“You are my student,” he once said as he sat behind me on my motorcycle, “the only one who has flooded me with questions. But you are not my disciple.”

Even today I can see him sitting there as we rode through the darkness. Only now do I realize how far that journey would eventually carry me.

Shastri ji dismantled capitalism before my eyes and expected that I would become a communist. But my questioning mind could not rest there either. If communism were the answer, how was one to account for the horrors committed in its name in China, Russia, and Vietnam? Having learned to look historically, I could now see a disturbing pattern. Whether feudalism, colonialism, imperialism, socialism, or capitalism, the same cycles of violence, exploitation, and injustice kept reappearing.

The answer was not in systems.

>*After millions of years we have not solved our problems: we are cutting each other’s throats, we are competing with each other, we hate each other, we want to be successful, the whole pattern is repeated from the time man began and we are still at it. Do what you will along this pattern and no human problem will be solved, whether it be political, religious or economic, because it is thought that is operating.*  
>*~ J. Krishnamurti, The Network of Thought*

After fifteen years of wandering—building and leaving institutions, eating with the poor, dining with the rich, being betrayed by those I trusted, and being rejected by some of those I had tried to help—I finally understood what had struck me as a twenty-year-old engineering student opening The Network of Thought in the college library.

I often wonder whether I could have understood those words in any other way.

## The Great Return
I closed down NEEV Vidyalaya. An educational institution, tethered to certifications from the state, no matter how well intentioned, ultimately serves the interests of the state more than those of the child.

My self-inquiry, which had begun with an explosion within me, had now encompassed the whole world.

>*The outward and the inward are the same movement; they are not different. It is one unitary movement, but we have divided it as the inner and the outer. By observing the outer clearly, you then discover the inner and then see that it is one movement.*  
>*— J. Krishnamurti, Total Freedom*

Gradually I began to see what he meant.

My thought and the world were not two different things. The world I had spent years trying to understand was, in countless ways, a reflection of thought itself. Liberation from suffering was not to be found by rearranging the world alone but by understanding the movement of thought.

I had stirred a great deal in the world. Now I resolved to discover the “way of heaven” within.

It was then that Advaita, the Indian tradition of non-duality, entered my life with the force of a thunderclap:

You are Brahman.

## Never Two
Nothing in my life had prepared me for what I was to discover.

In Advaita, enlightenment is often spoken of as a second birth. I had awakened from saṃsāra, the dream of birth and death, into that which had never been born — Brahman.

The world did not disappear. It became like a rainbow—radiantly present in all its colors, yet without independent substance.

*Saṃsāra is Nirvāṇa.*

And from within that rainbow, in my fiftieth year, came our son.

We fondly call him Adi — a nickname for Advait.

The day he was born, his mother updated her WhatsApp status:

Advait is not about becoming one. It is realizing that you were never two.

Adi and I often go out for walks together. He keeps flailing his little left hand in the air as though conducting an unseen orchestra. Squirrels chirp and dart up a palm tree. A dog yelps in the distance. An electric scooter glides past with its barely audible whirr. He follows it with his eyes for a while and then quietly looks into mine.

Never two.
