## The Call in the Night
It is past midnight. A pool of light from my desk lamp falls softly across the keyboard. Apart from the quiet clacking of the keys, the only other sound in the hushed silence is the chorus of crickets outside. My wife and our nine-month-old son are asleep in the other room. They had waited for me to come to bed, but like so many nights before, I remain awake in my fifty-first year, trying at last to give my life to a calling that has followed me for most of my life.

The stillness leaves little room for distraction. Doubts begin to gnaw at my sense of purpose. Am I too late in answering my call to be a writer? With half of my life behind me and the effervescence of youth long gone, do I still have enough time to fulfill the dream that has quietly accompanied me all these years?

Some callings arrive early. Others wait patiently until the noise of life begins to settle. Only now does my life seem to have made room for this one.

Looking back, I feel like a grizzled veteran returning from a long campaign. For three decades I fought to build a life outside the corporate world—a school for underprivileged children, a nonprofit, and a social enterprise. Each demanded everything I could give, and each fulfilled its purpose before I laid it down. They were the campaigns that finally brought me to this quiet room, this keyboard, and the calling that had been waiting for me all along.

Am I late?

## The Tyranny of Spring
Perhaps I am not the only one plagued by this question. Like me, there may be millions of others sighing quietly, wondering whether they have missed their moment. Child prodigies like Mozart composing music at eight or Rimbaud belting out his rebellion in teenage verse would embarrass even the young—what to speak of the grey hairs? Even in a genre like nonfiction, where one would expect experience to hold sway, Annie Dillard’s Pulitzer Prize-winning Pilgrim at Tinker Creek, written when she was just twenty-seven, unsettles that hope. Can the grey hairs still unleash a creative monster like the one Mary Shelley summoned in Frankenstein when she was just nineteen?

Youth—the salad days of life—abounds with energy, vitality, rebellion, and a mind not yet burdened by excessive self-criticism. A vast sea of time stretches before us, inviting us to experiment, flounder, fail, and begin again. In the autumn of life, when energy is measured and the mind mellowed by all that might have been but never was, the pen no longer glides across the page with quite the same abandon. Time, meanwhile, is no longer a luxury. What increasingly feels like borrowed time raises the stakes. There is little room left for experimentation, failure, and fresh beginnings. The sea that once invited reckless voyages now seems to ask for safe passage home.

The world around us does little to quiet these fears. Every year, lists like Forbes’ “30 Under 30” celebrate those who seem to have conquered the world before they have scarcely entered it. Social media only magnifies the spectacle. Young faces appear on magazine covers, fortunes are amassed before the first grey hair appears, and unknown creators awaken to millions of followers overnight. Every headline, every scrolling screen, every triumphant story seems to whisper the same command: bloom early.

Spring, it seems, is the only season the world knows how to celebrate.

## The Wisdom of Autumn
And yet, isn’t the autumn leaf beautiful in its colours? Don’t its colours speak of a wisdom that has seen so many seasons? This old master is rich with the memories of every summer sun it ever held. It may not possess the suppleness of youth, but within it has crystallized a wisdom that can bear the full freight of lived experience.

Cognitive psychologists Raymond Cattell and John Horn distinguish between two kinds of intelligence that follow radically different paths across a lifetime. Fluid intelligence typically peaks early, around the age of twenty, and gradually declines thereafter. It is the intelligence of rapid problem-solving, working memory, mental speed, and novel pattern recognition. It wins mathematics Olympiads and gives us child prodigies.

Our old master, however, returns to the earth the wisdom of every season it has lived through. Cattell and Horn describe this as crystallized intelligence"—the accumulated knowledge, patterns, and lived experience that only time can bestow. Literary essays, philosophical fiction, lyrical prose that weaves personal memory with universal insight, cultural synthesis — these are fields not of fluid cleverness but of crystallized wisdom.

This is not merely a comforting thought. Neuroscientist Elkhonon Goldberg, in The Wisdom Paradox (2005), argues that some forms of creative work require an aging mind to reach their fullest expression. As the years pass, the mind grows richer in crystallized intelligence, drawing upon a lifetime of remembered patterns to make fresh and unexpected associations. Some forms of creativity do not survive despite age. They ripen because of it.

After all, it seems there is another season in which creativity flowers. In Old Masters and Young Geniuses: The Two Life Cycles of Artistic Creativity (2006), University of Chicago economist David Galenson argues that there are two fundamentally different paths to innovation, each following its own rhythm across a lifetime. Old masters such as Michelangelo and Rembrandt were experimental innovators, advancing by trial and error and arriving at their greatest contributions only after decades of patient work. In contrast, conceptual innovators—Van Gogh, Herman Melville, and many others—often made sudden breakthroughs early in life by beginning with bold new ideas.

For Galenson, the difference is not merely one of age but of temperament. Young geniuses often begin with a clear vision and execute it boldly. Old masters proceed differently. They discover what they are trying to say by working patiently through years of trial, revision, and experience. Their masterpieces are not sudden revelations but slow ripenings.

Perhaps this is why the lyrical essay feels so at home in autumn. It is a form that thinks through writing rather than before it. It trusts perception more than certainty, lived experience more than abstract systems, and the slow accumulation of insight over the sudden brilliance of revelation.

## When Life Writes the Writer
Car mechanic José Saramago did not become a full-time writer until his fifties. His international breakthrough came with Baltasar and Blimunda, published in 1982 when he was sixty. Sixteen years later, at seventy-five, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature for works that, in the words of the Nobel Committee, “with parables sustained by imagination, compassion and irony continually enable us once again to apprehend an elusive reality.”

It is tempting to read this as an inspiring story of late success. But that would miss the deeper truth. Saramago did not suddenly bloom at sixty. By then, the roots had already spent decades growing unseen.

Before literature claimed him, life did. He worked as a mechanic and metalworker, became a publisher, journalist, and translator, and lost his position during Portugal’s political upheavals. Later, political controversy drove him into exile on the island of Lanzarote. These were not the years in which he wrote his great books. They were the years in which life was quietly writing him.

Psychologist Martin Seligman and others have described a phenomenon known as post-traumatic growth: the paradox that certain forms of psychological and creative flourishing emerge not despite profound disruption, but because of it. Saramago’s exile did not diminish his imagination; it deepened it. Removed from the noise of literary politics, he entered a deeper conversation with the human condition.

Blindness, one of his greatest novels, was written when he was seventy-three.

This is not a footnote to his life. It is the thesis.

## The Season of Seeing
Perhaps the deeper mistake lies not in how we think about creativity, but in how we think about time itself.

The very idea of a “late bloomer” belongs to a culture that imagines life as a straight line—one measured by milestones, productivity, and relentless achievement. But not every civilization has understood time in this way. The Vedic and Advaitic traditions imagine time not merely as chronological progression but as a rhythm of inner maturation, where wisdom ripens in its own season and the deepest flowering of life happens in the autumn.

Seen in this light, the Āśrama system of Vedic India—Brahmacharya, Gṛhastha, Vānaprastha, and Sannyāsa—is more than a social arrangement. It is a map of human maturation. Vānaprastha, the stage of the forest-dweller, need not be understood merely as a withdrawal from life but as a turning toward its depths, where the perceiver becomes most available to reality. The writer who enters this season with awareness awakens to a quality of perception unavailable to the young.

Perhaps the autumn leaf was never announcing its end. It was revealing, at last, everything the summer had quietly prepared it to become.

The older writer carries within them a library that no younger writer can access. The smell of monsoon on laterite stone, the texture of a particular grief, the quality of evening light in a place inhabited for decades—these cannot be recovered through research. They have become part of the writer’s way of seeing. The old master draws from precisely this archive.

There is another gift that sometimes arrives with age. After decades of trying on borrowed voices, some writers finally discover the cadence that had always been theirs. Saramago’s unmistakable stylistic signature—those marathon sentences, dialogue folded seamlessly into narration, and the narrator’s voice slipping almost imperceptibly into the consciousness of his characters—did not emerge at twenty-five. It arrived when he was fifty-eight.

But let us also be honest. For every Saramago who bloomed at sixty, there are thousands of people of equal—or greater—gift who spent their lives as mechanics, translators, teachers, farmers, or caregivers, and whose finest work remained unwritten because life never granted them the conditions in which it could emerge. Not every autumn becomes visible to the world. And not every late life produces a masterpiece.

## Not Every Autumn Is Witnessed
Hours have melted into the shadow of night, and the darkness breathes deeply as I write these final words. In this hermit’s silence, I watch them appear upon the screen—a flowering rising from roots that have quietly grown underground for decades. My eyes wander to the bookshelf where a row of journals rests, chronicling my life from my college days into my early forties.

>*If you don’t write your books, nobody else will do it for you. No one else has lived your life.*<p>
>*— José Saramago*

The question, for me now, is not whether I am too late to write. The question is whether anyone will read what I have written. And here I find myself returning to my roots—to Indian philosophy.

Lord Krishna tells Arjuna:

>*Your choice is in action alone, never in its fruits. Do not think yourself the author of the results of action, nor let your attachment be to inaction.*<p>
>*— Bhagavad Gita 2.47*

Not every autumn is witnessed. It is autumn nonetheless.
